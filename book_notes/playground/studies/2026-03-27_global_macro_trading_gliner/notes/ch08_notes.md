# GMT Chapter 8 Notes: Equities

## Core Concept and Author Intent

Chapter 8 treats equities as a macro expression vehicle rather than a pure stock-picking domain. Gliner's aim is to show how a macro trader should think about indices, sectors, derivative overlays, and broad valuation measures without pretending to have a single-name fundamental edge. The chapter argues that equity trading in macro is really about country choice, sector composition, and cycle-sensitive valuation.

## Core Mental Models

- Equity indices are macro baskets, not neutral market snapshots.
- Index composition matters because sector weights determine macro beta.
- Macro equity trading is usually top-down, not bottom-up.
- Volatility is both a hedge and a sentiment barometer.
- Sector leadership changes with the business cycle.
- No single valuation ratio is sufficient; multiple lenses should be combined.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- Start with the macro regime, then narrow to country, sector, and only then to a basket or index.
- Know whether an index is price-weighted, market-cap weighted, or otherwise constructed before using it as a signal.
- When expressing a thematic view, prefer baskets or ETFs to single names unless you truly understand idiosyncratic risk.
- Treat sector rotation as a regime tool, not a permanent style.
- Use VIX and other volatility products cautiously; they are powerful but can be structurally tricky.
- Compare valuation measures to both history and peers, not to arbitrary thresholds alone.
- Use credit, PMI, commodity prices, and consumer confidence as supporting equity indicators rather than pure macro trivia.

## Key Frameworks

### 1. Top-Down Equity Process

One of the clearest ideas in the chapter is the top-down sequence:

1. choose the global or regional macro view
2. identify the country or market likely to benefit
3. isolate the sectors that best express the view
4. decide whether to use an index, basket, ETF, or derivative overlay

This is the macro trader's alternative to deep single-name research.

### 2. Index and Sector Structure

The chapter emphasizes that major equity indices have very different exposures:

- some are more financial-heavy
- some are more technology-heavy
- some EM indices are strongly exposed to banks and commodities

That matters because two equity markets can both be "up" while expressing very different macro impulses.

### 3. Equity Expression Toolkit

Gliner reviews several ways to express equity views:

- index futures and outright indices
- stock baskets
- sector rotation
- ETFs
- ADRs
- VIX and volatility products
- variance swaps
- dividend swaps

The practical lesson is that expression choice changes both the beta you get and the risks you inherit.

### 4. Sector Rotation by Economic Cycle

The chapter uses classic cycle logic:

- defensive sectors tend to hold up better in contraction
- cyclical sectors do better in recovery and expansion

The main takeaway is not that rotation is mechanically stable, but that sector composition should reflect where the economy is in the cycle rather than a static equity preference.

### 5. Equity Valuation Stack

The chapter's valuation menu is broad:

- price-to-book
- dividend yield / price-to-dividend
- price-to-earnings and forward P/E
- price-to-free-cash-flow
- stock-market-cap-to-GDP
- commodity-price inflation pressure
- CDX high-yield as a risk appetite signal
- PMI
- Baltic Dry Index
- TRIN / ARMS
- consumer confidence
- AUD volatility as a risk proxy

The book is effectively saying that broad equity valuation should combine accounting ratios, macro breadth, credit stress, and sentiment.
