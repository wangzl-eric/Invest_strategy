# Report Digest: Bridgewater Associates — "Risk Parity Is about Balance" (2012)

- **Source:** https://www.bridgewater.com/research-and-insights/risk-parity-is-about-balance (via `book_notes/playground/papers/paper_notes_bridgewater2012.md`)
- **Type / issuer:** Buy-side research note (Bridgewater Associates, "All Weather" franchise)
- **Read on:** 2026-06-28
- **One-line claim:** A conventional 60/40 portfolio is ~90% equity risk; balancing *risk* (not capital) across asset classes and levering the low-volatility sleeve produces better risk-adjusted returns across all macro environments.
- **Decision it changes:** Size positions by risk contribution and lever bonds/commodities to parity rather than holding a capital-weighted 60/40 that is a levered equity bet in disguise.
- **Scores (1–5):** Credibility 4 · Relevance 5 · Actionability 5 — authoritative practitioner source and directly implementable, but it is the originator talking its own product book.

## 1. Executive summary (context · methodology · conclusion)
Bridgewater, the originator of the All Weather/risk-parity approach, published this note to argue that traditional 60/40 allocations are dangerously unbalanced — roughly 90% of their risk sits in equities because equities carry about three times the volatility of bonds, making 60/40 effectively a levered equity position. Methodologically the piece is a framework-and-decomposition argument rather than an empirical study: it decomposes portfolio risk by asset class, maps four asset classes onto a 2×2 of growth and inflation surprises (rising/falling), and shows each environment is "covered" by some asset, then volatility-weights positions and applies leverage to the low-volatility sleeves to equalize risk across the four quadrants. The conclusion is that allocating *risk* equally — about 25% to each macro environment — and using leverage to lift the Sharpe of low-vol assets delivers more consistent risk-adjusted returns than capital-weighted 60/40, because no single macro regime dominates the risk budget. The note's core theoretical claim is that leverage improves *Sharpe*, not merely return. It is a persuasive design argument whose central empirical assumption — that bonds reliably hedge equity risk — is asserted more than stress-tested.

## 2. Methodology & reasoning (≥ 1 paragraph)
The methodology is a risk-budgeting decomposition layered on a macro taxonomy, not a backtest with significance tests. The load-bearing observation is descriptive and quantitative: equity volatility ≈ 3× bond volatility, so a 60/40 capital split implies ≈90% of portfolio variance comes from equities. From this single fact the argument chains cleanly — if capital weights misrepresent risk, then balance must be defined on risk contributions; if risk should be balanced, then low-vol assets must be *levered* to contribute equally; and if returns are driven by growth and inflation surprises, then balancing across the four-quadrant environment map removes the regime concentration that makes 60/40 fragile. The reasoning is internally coherent and the volatility arithmetic is sound, but the statistics presented are almost entirely descriptive (volatility ratios, risk shares, quadrant mappings) rather than inferential — there are no out-of-sample tests, t-stats, or multiple-regime significance checks in the note itself, so the empirical strength rests on the framework's plausibility plus Bridgewater's track record. The key assumption that carries the whole conclusion is that asset-class correlations — especially the stock/bond hedge — stay close to their historical (often negative) values; the weakest link is precisely there, and it is also where the reader should discount for conflict of interest, since the note markets the firm's own All Weather product. The 2022 episode, when stocks and bonds fell together under an inflation shock and risk parity drew down hard, is the real-world counterexample that exposes this assumption — the correlation regime the framework relies on is itself state-dependent.
