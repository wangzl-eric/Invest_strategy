# ER Chapter 6 Notes: Behavioral Finance

## Core Concept

Chapter 6 provides the behavioral counterweight to Chapter 5. Ilmanen argues that persistent mispricing can exist because two conditions hold at the same time:

- investors exhibit systematic biases in beliefs and preferences
- rational arbitrageurs face limits that prevent them from fully correcting the resulting mispricing

## Author Intent

The authorial goal is not only to catalog anomalies, but to explain why they survive even in competitive, professionalized markets. Ilmanen is trying to show that inefficiency can remain economically meaningful even when many sophisticated investors are actively looking for it.

## Key Technicalities

- Limits to arbitrage:
  - real-world arbitrage is risky, capital-constrained, and often leveraged
  - mispricing can widen before it converges
  - delegated capital, margin calls, stop-loss rules, illiquidity, and forced redemptions all shorten the effective horizon of arbitrageurs
  - LTCM and later de-leveraging episodes are used as concrete examples
- Psychology taxonomy:
  - heuristic simplification and limited attention
  - availability and salience
  - representativeness and conservatism
  - overconfidence, hindsight, narrow framing, and related self-deception or preference distortions
- Prospect theory and salience:
  - loss aversion, nonlinear probability weighting, and the appeal of lottery-like payoffs help explain why some assets become overpriced despite poor long-run reward
  - the chapter also discusses salience-based mechanisms as an alternative microfoundation for distorted decision-making
- Momentum versus reversal:
  - behavioral models can generate short- and medium-term momentum alongside long-run reversal
  - Ilmanen reviews the standard sequence from underreaction to overreaction to correction across several well-known models

## Historical Evidence, Theories, and Forward-Looking Indicators

- The chapter treats speculative bubbles, value effects, momentum effects, and lottery-like overpricing as the main market applications of behavioral finance.
- The limits-to-arbitrage literature explains why these patterns can persist:
  - arbitrage is hardest when substitutes are poor
  - model uncertainty is high
  - capital is fragile
  - crowded trades face endogenous liquidation risk
- The chapter links long-run reversal to overreaction and short-horizon momentum to underreaction, delayed information processing, or feedback trading.
- Post-earnings-announcement drift, value-versus-growth effects, and momentum/reversal evidence are all discussed as examples of patterns that can be rationalized behaviorally.
- Forward-looking signals implied by the chapter are indirect but important:
  - leverage and crowding can signal fragile arbitrage conditions
  - extreme narratives or salient outcomes can distort pricing
  - perceived lottery features can attract buyers even when expected returns are poor

## Chapter Connections

- Chapter 6 completes the Part I theory split: Chapter 5 explains expected returns through rational pricing, while Chapter 6 explains why anomalies and mispricings may survive.
- The behavioral foundation here feeds directly into the later value, momentum, volatility, and carry chapters.
- The discussion also reinforces Chapter 1's warning that expected returns reflect both rational compensation and distorted investor demand.

## What Seems Immediately Testable with Available Data

- Reproduce momentum, reversal, and value effects across major asset classes and test their sensitivity to crowding and volatility regimes.
- Compare the performance of lottery-like equities versus less skewed peers to test the chapter's preference-distortion logic.
- Study whether momentum or value spreads become especially strong when funding stress or macro uncertainty is high.
- Examine event continuation effects such as earnings-related drift where sufficiently clean data are available.

## What Likely Requires External or Harder-to-Source Data

- Fund-level leverage, margin, redemption, and positioning data needed to observe limits to arbitrage directly.
- Investor-level behavioral or transaction data needed to validate specific psychological channels.
- Better salience, media, and narrative-attention datasets.

## Material Score

- Credibility: 5/5. The chapter is grounded in canonical behavioral-finance and limits-to-arbitrage literature and ties the ideas to real market episodes.
- Relevance: 5/5. It explains why many style premia and anomalies can persist despite widespread awareness.
- Actionability: 4/5. The empirical implications are useful, but several of the cleanest tests need external behavioral or fund-flow data.

## Open Questions and Things to Verify Empirically

- Which anomalies remain robust after controlling for crowding, leverage, and funding stress?
- How should we measure effective arbitrage capacity in a way that is useful for live strategy monitoring?
- Are salience and lottery-preference effects strong enough to matter outside equities, for example in credit, FX, or volatility markets?
- When do behavioral explanations add genuine forecasting value beyond rational bad-times and liquidity-premium models?
