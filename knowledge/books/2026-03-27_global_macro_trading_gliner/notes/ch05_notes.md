# GMT Chapter 5 Notes: Technical Analysis

## Core Concept and Author Intent

Chapter 5 treats technical analysis as a practical decision aid rather than a complete worldview. Gliner is not trying to prove that charts reveal deep truth; he is showing how technical tools give macro traders a disciplined way to enter, exit, size, and manage trades. The chapter is broad and eclectic, ranging from chart basics to Elliott Wave, seasonality, cycles, and crowd psychology. The strongest practitioner message is that technicals help structure risk even when one's fundamental story is correct only eventually.

## Core Mental Models

- Technical analysis is most valuable as a discipline tool.
- Charts compress price history into tradeable structure.
- Trend is the default organizing principle; many other indicators are secondary.
- Indicators are context-dependent: some work better in trend, others in range.
- Sentiment extremes and crowding matter because markets overshoot.
- Technical setups should improve risk/reward asymmetry, not replace macro reasoning.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- Match chart periodicity to holding horizon before drawing conclusions.
- Use trend, support/resistance, and moving averages before relying on more exotic indicators.
- Prefer technical tools that give clear invalidation levels.
- Treat oscillators as timing aids in range-bound markets, not as universal signals.
- Use positioning and sentiment to identify crowded trades or late-stage moves.
- Be cautious with parabolic markets: calling the top is difficult, but crowd psychology often becomes extreme near peaks.
- Use seasonality and cycle claims as weak priors that require confirmation from other evidence.

## Key Frameworks

### 1. Chart Taxonomy

The chapter reviews:

- line charts
- bar charts
- candlesticks
- point-and-figure charts
- logarithmic scaling

The practical framework is that different chart types expose different information density. Candles and bars help with entry/exit context; log scales help interpret long-run compounded moves.

### 2. Trend Framework

The most useful technical baseline in the chapter is:

- identify trend direction
- mark support and resistance
- watch for trend-line breaks
- use moving averages to formalize the trend state

This is closely aligned with Chapter 2's emphasis on risk/reward and stop placement.

### 3. Indicator Stack

Gliner groups common tools by function:

- moving averages and crossovers for trend state
- Bollinger Bands for overbought / oversold context
- MACD for momentum change
- RSI and stochastics for range-bound extremes

The chapter's implicit heuristic is to use a small stack of complementary indicators instead of hunting for perfect confirmation.

### 4. Pattern Recognition Layer

The pattern section covers:

- head and shoulders
- triple tops / bottoms
- triangles and continuation setups
- broadening formations

These are all ways of converting visual price structure into risk/reward-defined trade setups. The important practitioner lesson is not that the patterns are always true, but that they imply clear breakout and invalidation logic.

### 5. Sentiment and Regime-Extreme Layer

The most interesting non-chart parts of the chapter are:

- CFTC positioning
- parabolic rises
- seasonality
- long-cycle narratives
- crowd psychology via IPOs and magazine covers

These tools are best read as crowding and narrative saturation indicators rather than standalone signals.

## Chapter Connections

- Chapter 5 operationalizes Chapter 2's call for predefined entry, stop, and target logic.
- Trend-following and momentum ideas here connect directly to Chapter 6 systematic trading.
- Historical pattern recognition here is a visual cousin of Chapter 3 analog work.

## What Seems Immediately Testable with Available Data

- Moving-average trend systems across equity, rates, FX, and commodity futures.
- Bollinger, RSI, and stochastics in explicitly range-bound regimes.
- CFTC positioning as a sentiment or crowding feature.
- Seasonality studies in commodities, equities, and FX.
- Breakout and continuation setups with objective rules for entry and invalidation.

## What Likely Requires External or Harder-to-Source Data

- Clean magazine-cover or IPO-timing sentiment datasets.
- High-quality historical open-interest and positioning series for all markets.
- Robust cycle datasets that avoid hindsight-heavy interpretation.

## Material Score

- Credibility: 3/5. Some components are robust and widely used, while others, especially Elliott Wave and long-cycle claims, are far more contestable.
- Relevance: 4/5. Technical discipline is useful for macro trade expression even when one is not a pure technician.
- Actionability: 4/5. Many tools are testable, but the more subjective pattern frameworks require careful codification.

## Open Questions and Things to Verify Empirically

- Are technical tools best used as standalone signals or only as entry/exit overlays on macro views?
- How much of the value in technical analysis comes from risk control rather than forecasting?
- Can subjective tools like Elliott Wave be reduced into testable rules without destroying what technicians think makes them useful?
- Which sentiment indicators best identify late-stage crowding in global macro markets today?
