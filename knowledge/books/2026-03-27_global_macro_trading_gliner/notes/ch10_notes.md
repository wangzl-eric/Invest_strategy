# GMT Chapter 10 Notes: Commodities

## Core Concept and Author Intent

Chapter 10 argues that commodities are the purest macro markets because they force the trader to think in terms of scarce resources, physical constraints, and inventories. Gliner's main message is that commodity prices are not just "risk assets"; they are the direct outcome of supply, demand, storage, transport, seasonality, and political control. The chapter is trying to make the reader think like a physical-market observer before thinking like a chart trader.

## Core Mental Models

- Commodity prices are the visible output of physical supply-demand balance.
- Inventory is a state variable, not a footnote.
- The futures curve contains information about scarcity, storage, and convenience yield.
- Physical players often know more than purely financial traders.
- Commodities are high-volatility markets where microstructure matters more than in other macro products.
- Each commodity has its own economic logic; "commodities" is not a homogeneous asset class.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- Start every commodity view with supply, demand, and ending-stock maps.
- Distinguish long-term reserve stories from short-term production disruptions.
- Watch the futures curve shape; contango and backwardation are economic signals, not just pricing curiosities.
- In agriculture, respect crop calendars and weather risk more than broad macro narratives.
- In energy, spare capacity and cartel behavior can dominate standard supply-demand extrapolation.
- In precious metals, separate monetary-demand drivers from industrial-demand drivers.
- Size cautiously because commodity volatility can overwhelm a fundamentally correct thesis.

## Key Frameworks

### 1. Supply-Demand-Inventory Framework

The chapter's foundational model is simple:

- supply
- demand
- ending stock / inventory

That framework is what turns a commodity view from a vague macro opinion into a balance-sheet style question: what is available now, what is being consumed, and how much buffer remains?

### 2. Curve-Shape Framework

Contango and backwardation are presented as core tools:

- contango often reflects storage, financing, and transport costs
- backwardation often reflects near-term scarcity or convenience yield

The useful implication is that roll yield is not a technical nuisance. It is part of the economics of the trade.

### 3. Sub-Sector Commodity Map

The chapter organizes commodities into:

- energy
- precious metals
- industrial metals
- agriculture

That classification matters because each bucket has different dominant drivers. Oil is heavily geopolitical and cartel-influenced, gold is partly monetary, copper is growth-sensitive, and crops are calendar- and weather-sensitive.

### 4. Energy Framework

The energy section centers on:

- oil quality and grades
- producers, exporters, and consumers
- national oil companies
- OPEC and spare capacity
- natural gas supply changes from shale and drilling technology

The key practitioner lesson is that energy is not only about demand. It is also about who has the spare capacity and who can bring supply online.

### 5. Metals Framework

The metals sections split into two macro types:

- precious metals as monetary-confidence and reserve-diversification trades
- industrial metals as growth and construction-input trades

Gold is treated partly as anti-fiat money, while copper is treated as a real-economy barometer. That is a useful distinction because the same "commodity rally" can be driven by very different mechanisms.

### 6. Agriculture Framework

Agriculture is framed around:

- acreage
- yields
- weather
- crop calendars
- substitution and feedstock dynamics
- policy-driven demand such as ethanol

This is where the chapter is strongest. It makes clear that crop markets are not just about annual production totals; they are about timing, vulnerability windows, and how one crop's economics can affect another's acreage and pricing.

## What Seems Immediately Testable with Available Data

- Inventory and futures-curve signals for energy and agricultural commodities.
- Contango/backwardation as predictors of subsequent roll-adjusted returns.
- Commodity-linked cross-asset relationships such as copper-growth, gold-real-rates, and oil-FX.
- Crop-calendar seasonality studies for corn, wheat, soybeans, cotton, and coffee.
- OPEC spare-capacity or supply-shock event studies against oil prices and related assets.

## What Likely Requires External or Harder-to-Source Data

- High-quality inventory and warehouse data across commodities.
- Real-time physical flow data from producers, shippers, and traders.
- Agricultural weather, acreage, and yield microdata with revision history.
- Accurate NOC spare-capacity and export-infrastructure data.
