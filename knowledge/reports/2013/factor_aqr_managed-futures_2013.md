# Report Digest: AQR (Hurst, Ooi, Pedersen) — "Demystifying Managed Futures" (2013)

- **Source:** SSRN 2324288; *Journal of Investment Management* 11(3), 42–58 (via `knowledge/papers/paper_notes_hurst2013.md`)
- **Type / issuer:** Practitioner research note (AQR Capital Management)
- **Read on:** 2026-06-28
- **One-line claim:** The managed-futures / CTA industry's returns are almost entirely reproduced by a simple time-series-momentum (trend) rule applied across asset classes, and the premium is robust over 135 years with genuine crisis-alpha.
- **Decision it changes:** Treat trend-following as a cheap, transparent crisis hedge to hold against a long equity book, rather than paying for opaque "CTA skill."
- **Scores (1–5):** Credibility 5 · Relevance 5 · Actionability 5 — long history, simple replicable rule, broad asset coverage; mild discount because AQR runs trend products.

## 1. Executive summary (context · methodology · conclusion)
AQR published this note to demystify the managed-futures industry by showing that CTA returns are not idiosyncratic manager skill but a single, simple systematic premium. Methodologically the authors build a 135-year (1880–2012), 67-market dataset spanning equities, bonds, currencies, and commodities, define a time-series-momentum signal as the sign of each market's trailing 12-month return, size positions to a constant volatility target, net out estimated transaction costs, and then regress live CTA index returns on this synthetic strategy. The conclusion is that the simple trend rule earns a Sharpe of ~0.7 over the full sample (~0.9 post-1985), performs best precisely during the worst equity drawdowns (1929–32, 2000–02, 2008), and explains the CTA industry with an \$R^2 > 0.9\$ — so trend-following is both the engine of managed futures and a genuine portfolio diversifier rather than a disguised momentum-on-equities bet. The breadth of the sample and the crisis-period behavior are what make the claim hard to dismiss as data mining.

## 2. Methodology & reasoning (≥ 1 paragraph)
The method is a long-horizon, multi-asset backtest paired with a replication regression, and its credibility rests mostly on sample breadth rather than on a single significance test. The load-bearing evidence is twofold: first, the strategy survives across 67 markets and 135 years — many independent regimes, which directly attacks the data-snooping objection that modern-era trend results could be a fluke of one bull market; second, the high \$R^2\$ of CTA returns on the synthetic signal makes the inference "trend *is* what CTAs do" quantitatively, not just narratively. The reasoning chains tightly: observe that a 12-month return sign predicts the next month across asset classes → build vol-scaled positions so no single market dominates → show the aggregate Sharpe and, crucially, its *negative-to-zero* correlation with equities during crashes → infer that the return is a distinct, persistent premium with hedge value, not levered equity beta. The statistics mix descriptive (Sharpe levels, \$R^2\$, drawdown-period returns) with the implicit inferential weight of out-of-sample persistence across decades; the strongest single fact is the crisis-period outperformance, because it rules out the most damaging alternative explanation (that trend is just another risk-on factor). The key assumptions are that early cash/forward data faithfully proxy tradable futures returns and that the modest, estimated transaction costs hold at scale — both are where the result is most vulnerable, since real-time slippage, capacity limits, and post-2013 trend underperformance (a crowding/decay concern the 2012-vintage note cannot address) could compress the live premium well below the backtested Sharpe. As an AQR note promoting a strategy the firm sells, the framing is favorable, but the simplicity and transparency of the rule make the claim unusually checkable.
