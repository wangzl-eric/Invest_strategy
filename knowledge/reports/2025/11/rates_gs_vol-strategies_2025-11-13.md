# Report Digest: Goldman Sachs — A Macro User's Guide to Interest Rates Volatility Strategies (2025)

- **Source:** `knowledge/reports/2025/11/rates_gs_vol-strategies_2025-11-13.pdf`
- **Type / issuer:** Sell-side research (Global Markets Analyst) — Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Global Interest Rates Strategy (Bill Zu, with George Cole, William Marshall, Simon Freycenet, Friedrich Schaper)
- **Read on:** 2026-07-31 (report published 13 November 2025, 10:16am EST)
- **One-line claim:** Raw valuation signals for rates volatility (implied-vs-recent-realized ratios, IV z-scores) are poor timing tools for volatility-selling strategies, but a macro-fundamentals fair-value model (forecast dispersion for growth/inflation/policy, plus distance from the neutral policy rate) explains implied vol well and — combined with a momentum overlay — meaningfully improves the risk/reward of harvesting the volatility risk premium; a blended short-gamma/long-vega strategy further improves that profile and offers a genuinely low-correlation diversifier for multi-asset portfolios.
- **Decision it changes:** Whether to time rates-vol-selling strategies off raw valuation (cheap/rich implied vol vs. its own history) versus a macro-conditioned fair-value model, and whether to add a blended vol-carry overlay as a diversifying sleeve in a multi-asset book rather than relying on outright short-gamma exposure alone.

R² across tenors) rather than a described heuristic, and a long, multi-market sample (since 2003); actionability is capped because implementing any of it requires the platform to price and P&L options (delta-hedged straddles, gamma/vega), which does not exist here beyond an exploratory notebook, and because the macro fair-value model itself is proprietary and not reproducible from data in this platform.

## 1. Executive summary
Goldman Sachs' Global Interest Rates Strategy team (Bill Zu) publishes this piece on 13 November 2025 to give investors a framework for interpreting how rates volatility risk is priced and for deciding when and how to harvest the volatility risk premium, at a moment when implied volatility across major asset classes sits near the low end of its historical range despite perceptions of elevated macro uncertainty. The methodology combines a long-run empirical study of the volatility risk premium (implied-vs-realized ratios since 2003, across USD/EUR/GBP/JPY rates and multiple tenors), a test of raw valuation signals as return predictors, an estimated macro fair-value model regressing implied vol on consensus forecast dispersion and the policy-rate cycle, and a portfolio-construction comparison of short-gamma, long-vega, and blended strategies against equities, bonds, and a 60/40 mix. GS concludes that raw valuation signals do not reliably predict vol-selling returns, but valuation measured against a macro-implied fair value — especially when combined with recent momentum and conditioned on the growth/policy regime — does, and that a vol-weighted blend of short-gamma and long-vega strategies offers a moderate return/risk profile with low correlation to traditional portfolios, making it a credible alternative-risk-premia overlay.

## 2. Methodology & reasoning (PEE blocks + bulleted causal chains)

- **Point:** A genuine, harvestable volatility risk premium exists in rates — implied vol has historically exceeded subsequent realized vol by 10–20% on average.
  **Evidence:** Ratio of implied-to-subsequently-realized vol on ATM straddles averages ~1.1–1.6x across 2y/5y/10y/30y tails since 2003 (Exhibit 3), richer for shorter-dated tails (Exhibit 4); return-to-vol ratios of delta-hedged short-dated (1m10y/1m30y) straddle-selling in US/EU/UK cluster around 0.5–0.7, comparable to buy-and-hold S&P 500 (~0.71), the Bloomberg Treasury Agg (~0.62), and a 60/40 portfolio (~0.84) (Exhibit 5).
  **Chain:** Structural end-user demand for crash protection embeds an insurance premium in option prices → investors may also structurally overestimate crash probabilities (cited: Goetzmann et al. 2016) → both effects push implied vol above what subsequently realizes → selling delta-hedged straddles harvests exactly that implied-minus-realized spread → **historically, this has produced return-to-vol ratios on par with buying and holding equities or bonds.**
  **Read:** Descriptive base-rate evidence over a long (20+ year), multi-currency sample — reasonably robust. But "return-to-vol on par with buy-and-hold" says nothing about the *shape* of the return distribution (see Weakest link below); no skew or drawdown statistic is shown anywhere in the report for any vol-selling series.

- **Point:** Contrary to common practice, raw valuation signals — implied vol's level versus recent realized or versus its own history — are poor, unreliable predictors of returns to trading volatility.
  **Evidence:** Exhibit 6 (implied-to-recent-realized ratio vs. subsequent realized-vs-implied outcome) and Exhibit 7 (IV z-score on a 1-year lookback vs. the same outcome) both show a scatter close to flat/noisy across the bulk of observations; the report states predictability appears only in the tails (extreme cheap or rich IV) and even there is "driven by only a handful of episodes."
  **Chain:** The intuitive rule — "IV is cheap vs. its own history, so buy vol / don't sell" — implies a clear negative slope in Exhibits 6–7 → the actual scatter is nearly flat outside the extremes → **raw valuation, useful in equities and bonds, does not transfer reliably to rates-vol timing**, and even the tail-predictability GS does report is explicitly caveated as resting on a small number of episodes.
  **Read:** This is the report's most valuable methodological contribution — a stated negative result about a signal practitioners intuitively reach for, volunteered rather than glossed over, with its own small-sample caveat attached. It is also the premise that motivates the rest of the report (why a *macro* model is needed instead).

- **Point:** A macro-fundamentals fair-value model — implied vol regressed on consensus forecast dispersion (growth, inflation, policy) plus controls, and on distance from the neutral policy rate — explains implied vol's level well at short tenors/expiries, and deviations from that fair value (especially combined with momentum) predict vol-selling returns better than raw valuation alone.
  **Evidence:** Regression adjusted R² by expiry/tenor (Exhibit 11) runs roughly 0.7–0.85 for short expiries (1m–1y) across most tenors, but degrades to roughly 0.3–0.4 for the longest tails (30y tenor, 10y+ expiry); current US average implied vol sits at the low end of the model's fair-value range (Exhibit 8); vol-selling risk/reward improves when implied vol is rich vs. this fair value (Exhibit 12, a noisy but upward-sloping scatter, subsequent 3m return-to-vol ranging roughly −6 to +12) and improves further when vol is "high and falling" rather than "high and rising" (Exhibit 13, return-to-vol ratio rising from ~0.2 in the cheapest quintile to ~1.9 in the richest-and-falling quintile); performance is also conditioned on regime — best in modest-to-high growth with loose-to-neutral policy (Exhibit 14) and meaningfully worse when the yield curve sits within ±50bp of flat, i.e., near perceived policy "neutral" (Exhibit 15, return-to-vol ratio roughly 0.1–0.3 in that band vs. ~1.1 when the curve is >100bp from flat).
  **Chain:** Distance from neutral policy (a compressed forward curve) → the market prices in less uncertainty about the path to normalization → implied vol trades structurally lower and closer to fair value → **the risk-premium buffer thins, so vol-selling has less cushion and a materially worse return-to-vol ratio exactly when the curve is flattest** — this is presented as the mechanism behind Exhibit 15, and it is the same mechanism (curve position → vol pricing) used earlier to explain why belly vol richens as the curve normalizes (Exhibit 10).
  **Read:** The report's load-bearing inferential claim, and the only genuinely estimated model in the piece (reported adj. R² across a grid of tenors/expiries) — but the strong-fit claim ("macro explains implied vol pricing") is scope-limited: the R² is materially weaker at the longest tenors and expiries, so the model's explanatory power is really a short-tenor/short-expiry result being generalized in the prose to "the surface" more broadly than Exhibit 11 itself supports.

- **Point:** Blending short-gamma (carry) with long-vega (roll-down/convexity) improves the risk/reward profile relative to either alone, and the blend is a genuine low-correlation diversifier versus equities, bonds, and a 60/40 portfolio.
  **Evidence:** Short gamma alone: ~6.7% annualized return / ~11.2% annualized vol; long vega alone: ~2% return / ~4% vol; a vol-weighted blend: ~6.4% return at only ~6.7% vol (Exhibit 16) — most of short-gamma's return retained at roughly 60% of its standalone volatility. Monthly-return correlations (Exhibit 17): short gamma ≈ +0.3–0.4 vs. S&P 500 and a 60/40 portfolio (i.e., procyclical — it loses exactly when equities do), slightly negative vs. Treasuries; long vega ≈ 0 to slightly negative vs. S&P 500/60-40, slightly positive vs. Treasuries; the blend sits close to zero across all three.
  **Chain:** Short gamma is procyclical because vol spikes coincide with equity selloffs → long vega is the natural offset, since it profits from vol spikes via convexity → vol-weighting the two together preserves most of the carry return while cutting realized volatility from ~11% to ~7% → the blended series' correlation to stocks/bonds/60-40 collapses toward zero → **the blend, not either leg alone, is what earns the "alternative risk premia" / genuine diversifier label.**
  **Read:** Solid empirical portfolio-construction evidence (real annualized return/vol/correlation statistics over the 2003+ sample) — but exactly like Pillar 1, none of the three series (short gamma, long vega, blend) is shown with a skewness, max-drawdown, or tail-loss statistic, so "improves the risk/reward profile" is demonstrated only in mean-variance terms, not in terms of the tail behavior that actually differentiates these strategies from buy-and-hold equities/bonds.

**Weakest link:** Every return series in the report (Exhibits 5, 12, 13, 16, 17) is characterized only by annualized return, annualized vol, and return-to-vol/correlation ratios — never by skewness, kurtosis, or maximum drawdown. Short-gamma and vol-carry strategies are canonically negatively skewed (small steady premium income, punctuated by rare large losses when realized vol spikes past implied), which is precisely the mechanism the report's own language half-describes ("returns... primarily determined by the spread between implied volatility and what is subsequently realized") without ever quantifying the tail. A strategy can look identical to buy-and-hold on a return-to-vol basis while carrying dramatically worse crash risk — the report's central portfolio-construction recommendation rests on a statistic (mean-variance return-to-vol) that is least informative for exactly this strategy family.

$$\text{P\&L}_{\text{short gamma, delta-hedged}} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\,S^2\,\big(\sigma_{\text{IV}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{RV}}^2\big)\,dt$$

where $\Gamma$ is the option's gamma, $S$ is the underlying rate/price level, $\sigma_{\text{IV}}$ is the volatility priced into the option at inception, $\sigma_{\text{RV}}$ is volatility subsequently realized, and $dt$ is the time increment over which the position is held and re-hedged. Economically: a delta-hedged short-gamma position earns a small, steady premium each day realized vol stays below implied (∝ the variance gap), but bleeds losses at a rate that scales with $\Gamma$ — which itself typically rises as an option approaches expiry or the underlying approaches the strike — exactly when realized vol spikes above implied. This identity is the mechanical reason the "weakest link" above matters: it is a structurally asymmetric payoff (steady small gains, occasional large losses) that a return-to-vol ratio, computed from the same $\Gamma(\sigma_{IV}^2-\sigma_{RV}^2)$ realizations, does not by itself distinguish from a symmetric one.
